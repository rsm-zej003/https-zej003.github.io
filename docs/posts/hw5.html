Introduction

A brand manager wants to improve customer satisfaction. She has survey data on nine product attributes — trust, build quality, ease of use, and six more — along with an overall satisfaction rating from each respondent. The obvious first step is to ask: which of these attributes actually drive satisfaction?

This is harder than it sounds. When attributes are positively correlated with each other (customers who trust a brand tend to find it appealing too), a simple ranking by correlation gives a distorted picture. Regression can help but produces coefficients that are sensitive to multicollinearity. The research community has converged on a toolkit of key driver analysis methods, each making different trade-offs between simplicity, interpretability, and statistical rigor.

This post applies six methods to the same dataset and compares what each tells us:

Method What it measures
Pearson correlation Raw linear association with satisfaction
Standardised regression coefficient Unique linear contribution, accounting for other drivers
Drop-one (usefulness) Unique contribution to R² when a driver is removed
Shapley values Fair allocation of R² using cooperative game theory
Johnson’s relative weights Orthogonal-rotation decomposition of R²
Random forest Gini decrease Non-linear importance from an ensemble of trees

Six Methods, One Dataset

1 · Pearson Correlation

The starting point: how strongly does each driver correlate with satisfaction?

pearson = {pr: stats.pearsonr(df[pr], y)[0] for pr in PREDICTORS}

Pearson correlation is easy to compute and explain, but it ignores that drivers share variance. If impact and trust are correlated, their individual correlations with satisfaction overlap — both appear important even when only one is causally driving the outcome.

2 · Standardised Regression Coefficient

Running a multiple regression with all drivers simultaneously controls for overlap. The standardised beta (regressing z-scored satisfaction on z-scored drivers) tells us how many standard deviations satisfaction changes per standard-deviation increase in each driver, holding the other drivers fixed.

std_beta = dict(zip(
    PREDICTORS,
    LinearRegression().fit(Xs, ys).coef_
))

r2_full = LinearRegression().fit(X, y).score(X, y)
print(f"Full-model R² = {r2_full:.4f}")
Full-model R² = 0.1090

Standardised betas correctly account for multicollinearity, but they can be unstable when drivers are highly correlated — small data changes can flip signs and magnitudes.

3 · Drop-One (Usefulness)

For each driver, we measure how much R² drops when that driver is removed from the full model. This quantity, sometimes called usefulness, isolates the unique predictive contribution of each variable.

drop_one_raw = {}
for i, pr in enumerate(PREDICTORS):
    X_drop = np.delete(X, i, axis=1)
    r2_drop = LinearRegression().fit(X_drop, y).score(X_drop, y)
    drop_one_raw[pr] = r2_full - r2_drop

drop_one_pct = {pr: v / sum(drop_one_raw.values()) * 100
                for pr, v in drop_one_raw.items()}

Drop-one is the most conservative measure of importance: it only credits a driver for what it contributes uniquely, beyond all other drivers. Highly redundant drivers — those whose predictive information is duplicated elsewhere — score near zero, even if they correlate strongly with satisfaction on their own.

4 · Shapley Values

Shapley values originate in cooperative game theory. Each driver is a “player,” the team’s joint payoff is R², and Shapley values divide that payoff fairly — accounting for every possible order in which drivers could enter the model.

For driver \(j\), its Shapley value is the average of its marginal contributions across all \(p!\) orderings:

\[ \phi_j = \frac{1}{p!}\sum_{\text{all orderings}} \bigl[R^2(S^{j}_{+}) - R^2(S^{j}_{-})\bigr] \]

where \(S^{j}_{+}\) is the set of drivers entering before \(j\) plus \(j\) itself, and \(S^{j}_{-}\) excludes \(j\).

An equivalent, computationally convenient form averages over all subsets rather than orderings:

\[ \phi_j = \sum_{S \subseteq P \setminus \{j\}} \frac{|S|!\,(p-|S|-1)!}{p!}\,\bigl[R^2(S \cup \{j\}) - R^2(S)\bigr] \]

With \(p = 9\) drivers there are \(2^9 = 512\) subsets to evaluate — small enough to do exactly.

# Pre-compute R² for every subset of drivers (512 total)
r2_cache = {}
for k in range(p + 1):
    for S in combinations(range(p), k):
        if k == 0:
            r2_cache[S] = 0.0
        else:
            r2_cache[S] = LinearRegression().fit(X[:, list(S)], y).score(
                              X[:, list(S)], y)

# Compute each driver's Shapley value
shapley_raw = {}
for j, pr in enumerate(PREDICTORS):
    others = [i for i in range(p) if i != j]
    sv = 0.0
    for k in range(p):
        for S in combinations(others, k):
            weight  = math.factorial(k) * math.factorial(p - k - 1) / math.factorial(p)
            S_plus  = tuple(sorted(S + (j,)))
            sv     += weight * (r2_cache[S_plus] - r2_cache[S])
    shapley_raw[pr] = sv

print(f"Shapley values sum to: {sum(shapley_raw.values()):.4f}  (R² = {r2_full:.4f})")
shapley_pct = {pr: v / sum(shapley_raw.values()) * 100
               for pr, v in shapley_raw.items()}
Shapley values sum to: 0.1090  (R² = 0.1090)

By construction the Shapley values sum exactly to R² — they partition the explained variance cleanly across all drivers. Shared variance is divided proportionally: if impact and trust jointly explain 10 pp of R² and each contributes equally in isolation, each receives 5 pp.

5 · Johnson’s Relative Weights

Johnson (2000) proposed an elegant closed-form approximation to Shapley values. The idea: rotate the predictor space into orthogonal components, regress satisfaction on those components, then rotate the R² contributions back to the original predictors.

Algorithm:

  1. Eigendecompose the predictor correlation matrix: \(\mathbf{R}_{XX} = \mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{P}^\top\)
  2. Construct orthogonal predictors: \(\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{X}_\text{std}\,\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1/2}\)
  3. Regress \(y\) on \(\mathbf{Z}\): each column \(k\) gets correlation \(r_k\) with \(y\)
  4. Map \(r_k^2\) back through the rotation: \(\varepsilon_j = \sum_k P_{jk}^2 \cdot r_k^2\)
R_xx = np.corrcoef(Xs.T)                    # predictor correlation matrix
eigvals, eigvecs = np.linalg.eigh(R_xx)    # eigendecomposition
Z = Xs @ eigvecs @ np.diag(eigvals**(-0.5)) # orthogonal components

# r²(Z_k, y_std) for each component
r2_comp = np.array([np.corrcoef(Z[:, k], ys)[0, 1]**2 for k in range(p)])

# Map back to original predictors: epsilon_j = sum_k P_{jk}^2 * r2_comp[k]
johnson_raw = {pr: float((eigvecs[i]**2) @ r2_comp)
               for i, pr in enumerate(PREDICTORS)}

print(f"Johnson weights sum to: {sum(johnson_raw.values()):.4f}  (R² = {r2_full:.4f})")
johnson_pct = {pr: v / sum(johnson_raw.values()) * 100
               for pr, v in johnson_raw.items()}
Johnson weights sum to: 0.1090  (R² = 0.1090)

Because the rotation distributes variance across all components, Johnson weights tend to be more uniform than Shapley values when predictors share a large common factor.

6 · Random Forest Gini Decrease

The first five methods all assume a linear relationship between drivers and satisfaction. A random forest makes no such assumption: it builds an ensemble of decision trees, and variable importance is measured by the total decrease in Gini impurity (for classification) or variance (for regression) when a variable is used to split nodes, averaged over all trees.

rf = RandomForestClassifier(
    n_estimators=500, random_state=42, n_jobs=-1
)
rf.fit(X, y.astype(int))
rf_gini_raw = dict(zip(PREDICTORS, rf.feature_importances_))
rf_gini_pct = {pr: v * 100 for pr, v in rf_gini_raw.items()}   # already sum to 1

The Gini decrease captures non-linear and interaction effects invisible to regression-based methods. It also tends toward equal sharing when variables can substitute for each other in a tree split.

Discussion

Three Tiers of Drivers

All six methods consistently identify the same top tier: Impactful and Trust are the primary drivers of satisfaction, with Service in a clear third place. The remaining six attributes fill a lower tier, with Popular and Rewarding consistently at the bottom.

The differences between methods appear in the magnitudes and the sharpness of the contrast between top and bottom drivers.

Why Methods Disagree

Drop-one is the most extreme. It credits Impact with 43% of all importance, Trust with 31%, and Service with 18% — leaving the remaining six attributes sharing less than 8% combined. This reflects its design: drop-one measures unique contribution. A driver that is correlated with other important drivers receives almost no credit, even if it is genuinely associated with satisfaction.

Shapley gives a balanced but differentiated picture. By averaging contributions across all possible entry orders, Shapley shares credit for overlapping variance. Impact (21.6%), Trust (19.8%), and Service (16.9%) still lead, but the gap to the middle tier is much smaller. This is the method that most closely resembles what a fair “audit” of R² should look like.

Johnson’s relative weights are the flattest. The orthogonal-rotation approach distributes weight more uniformly when predictors share a large common factor. Here the nine binary attributes are moderately correlated (all positive product-quality signals), so Johnson assigns 8–13% to each driver — almost no information for distinguishing primary from secondary drivers.

The random forest is even flatter. The Gini-decrease rankings (9–13%) are nearly uniform, suggesting that the tree ensemble can use any of the nine attributes interchangeably to split nodes. This is consistent with a data-generating process that has a strong common factor (overall brand quality) captured redundantly by all nine items.

What Each Method Is Good For

Goal Best method
Quick descriptive summary Pearson r
Controlling for redundancy (unique contribution) Drop-one
Fair attribution accounting for shared variance Shapley values
Fast approximation of Shapley Johnson relative weights
Non-linear / interaction effects RF Gini decrease

For this dataset, the three linear methods that correct for multicollinearity (standardised beta, drop-one, Shapley) converge on the same story: Impact and Trust are the priority levers for improving satisfaction. Service matters too but is a distant third. The remaining six attributes are unlikely to move the needle if resources are limited.

The RF Gini result serves as a useful robustness check: even a non-linear model with 500 trees finds it difficult to distinguish the drivers, which suggests the true signal in this data is modest (R² ≈ 0.11) and spread across correlated attributes rather than concentrated in one or two.
